How to keep track of books you've lent out

Every book lover has a gap on the shelf and a vague memory of lending something to someone. Lending books is one of the nice things about owning them, and losing them is the price. A small system takes most of the sting out. Here's what to record, a few easy ways to do it, and how to ask for a book back without awkwardness.

What to write down

You only need four things. If you capture these at the moment you hand the book over, you'll almost always get it back.

  • Which book. Title and author is enough, plus the edition if you own several copies.
  • Who has it. A name, and if it's a friend of a friend, how to reach them.
  • When you lent it. People forget how long it's been, and so do you.
  • When it's due back. Optional, but a date turns "whenever" into something concrete. A few weeks is a common choice for a novel.

A short note is useful too: "pages 40 to 60 are folded, that's fine" or "lent for book club".

Simple ways to keep a lending record

A notebook by the shelf

Reliable and low-tech, as long as you remember to write in it. The downside: you have to be at home to read it.

An index card in the gap

Slip a card with the borrower's name and date into the space on the shelf. You'll see it every time you look at the shelf. You can lose the card, and it doesn't help when you're away from home.

A photo

Take a picture of the borrower holding the book. It's cheap, fast and mildly amusing, but it's hard to search later.

A reminder or calendar entry

Set a reminder for the due date: "Ask Sam about the book". It handles the follow-up but not the record of what's out.

A catalog that tracks loans

If your library is already cataloged, the neatest approach is to record the loan against the book itself, so you can see at a glance everything that is out and who has it.

How to ask for a book back

  • Ask early and lightly. "Did you get a chance to start it?" is easier than "where's my book?"
  • Offer a reason. "I'm in the mood to re-read it" is true, kind and clear.
  • Make returning easy. Offer to pick it up, or suggest a time you'll both be at the same place.
  • Don't lend what you can't replace. For a signed copy or a first edition, offer a paperback instead, or tell them about it rather than lending it.
  • If it's gone for good, close the loan in your list rather than leaving it as "out" forever, and consider replacing it. A second-hand copy is often only a few dollars.

Common mistakes

The biggest is relying on memory. The second is lending without a date, so the loan drifts on indefinitely. The third is recording the loan in one place and the book in another, so you can never see them together. Keep one list for everything that's out.

Doing it with Shelfkeeper

Shelfkeeper keeps loans attached to the books in your library. Lending is free. The one Pro feature in this area is the due-date reminder.

  1. Open the book in your Library and tap Lend This Book.
  2. In Who's borrowing it?, type the borrower's name. If you've lent to someone before, their name appears as a button to tap.
  3. Leave Due date on and pick the day under Due back (it starts three weeks from today), or switch it off for an open-ended loan.
  4. Optionally turn on Remind me on the day to get a notification at 10 AM on the due date. This is part of Shelfkeeper Pro, and the notification stays on your iPhone.
  5. Add a note if you want, then tap Lend.
  6. Open the Lent Out tab any time to see every book that's out. Overdue loans are listed first, then the soonest due.
  7. When the book comes back, swipe left on it in Lent Out and tap Returned, or open the book and tap Mark as Returned. Switch the picker at the top to Returned to see your lending history and who tends to borrow what.
